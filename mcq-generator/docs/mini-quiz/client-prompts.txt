AO2
GCSE
Using only the scientific knowledge contained in the supplied textbook screenshot, create 6
GCSE Science AO2 application questions and a student-friendly mark scheme.
PURPOSE OF THE QUESTIONS
These questions will form the AO2 section of a mini-quiz completed after a student has revised
this page.
Their purpose is to give students controlled practice at USING and UNDERSTANDING the
scientific knowledge they have just revised in different ways.
They should act as a stepping stone between recalling knowledge and later completing full
exam questions.
They are NOT intended to reproduce the exact distribution or difficulty of AO2 questions found
in an exam. For example, even where a topic is commonly assessed through calculations in
exams, these questions can still assess whether students understand and can apply the
underlying science.
WHAT THE QUESTIONS SHOULD ASSESS
Focus primarily on students applying the scientific ideas/content they have just revised.
Treat anything taught on the supplied page as part of the content. For example, if the page
teaches a practical method, working-scientifically idea, technique or procedure, students can be
asked to apply it.
Do not require substantial scientific knowledge from other topics that is not contained in the
supplied page.
Maths, equations, numerical information, graphs or data may be used where they provide a
useful way for students to apply the science they have just revised.
For example, students may use a mathematical relationship qualitatively to predict what
happens when a variable changes.
However, do not make handling, solving or manipulating maths the main skill being assessed.
Avoid substantial calculations, multi-stage mathematical problems or questions where success
primarily depends on mathematical technique rather than understanding the science.
APPLICATION TYPES
Create genuine AO2 questions. Students should have to take something they have learned from
the page and USE it, rather than simply recall, state or describe what the page says.
Use the following forms of application where they genuinely fit the supplied content:
• NOVEL CONTEXT – apply learned knowledge to a new example or situation.
• DEVELOP FURTHER – use a learned principle or relationship to work out something that was
not explicitly taught.
• MAKE SENSE OF INFORMATION – use learned science to understand, interpret or explain
simple information supplied in the question.
• MAKE LINKS – connect two or more scientific ideas where the knowledge required for the link
is contained within the supplied revision content.
Select the application types that best suit the scientific content rather than forcing equal
numbers of each type.
Where the content genuinely allows, use at least 3 different application types across the 6
questions. Do not force an application type where it does not fit naturally.
BREADTH AND VARIETY
Across the 6 questions, maximise the range of scientific ideas and relationships from the
supplied content that students have to apply.
Do not repeatedly test the same underlying scientific idea or relationship merely by changing the
surface context.
Where the page contains enough content, each question should assess a meaningfully different
piece, relationship or combination of the revised knowledge.
It is acceptable to revisit an important idea where it is being applied in a meaningfully different
way.
STEPPING-STONE DIFFICULTY
These should be stepping-stone AO2 questions rather than full exam-level AO2 questions.
Each question should:
• require genuine application of the revised knowledge;
• use a short, clear context;
• normally require only one main application or a short reasoning chain;
• make the relevant scientific idea reasonably easy to identify;
• contain little or no irrelevant information;
• rely primarily on the content the student has just revised;
• use concise, accessible wording.
Some questions may be relatively close to AO1, provided the student still has to apply the
knowledge to a situation rather than simply reproduce it.
Other questions can require greater transfer or connection between ideas.
There does NOT need to be a fixed progression in difficulty from Question 1 to Question 6.
Prioritise a useful variety of applications over arranging the questions from easiest to hardest.
Do not create difficulty simply by making contexts longer, adding irrelevant information, requiring
substantial unrevised knowledge or creating unnecessarily long reasoning chains.
The aim is to preserve genuine AO2 application while reducing the contextual, synoptic and
multi-step complexity students are likely to encounter later in full exam questions.
QUESTION QUALITY
Every question must contain enough information for one scientifically defensible answer to
follow from the supplied revision content.
Do not rely on unstated assumptions about the scenario.
Provide enough information for students to identify the relevant science, but avoid unnecessarily
stating or strongly implying the conclusion they are being asked to reach.
Before finalising each question, check that:
• the answer is supported by the supplied revision content;
• the question genuinely requires application rather than simple recall;
• the scenario contains enough information to reach the intended answer;
• no substantial knowledge from outside the supplied content is required;
• the wording does not unnecessarily give away the answer.
QUESTION OUTPUT
Create exactly 6 questions.
Use a mixture of appropriate question styles such as explain, predict, suggest, compare,
determine or interpret where these genuinely suit the content.
Do not use multiple-choice questions.
Keep the questions concise.
First determine the minimum scientific reasoning needed for a complete answer to each
question. Then assign the number of marks from the genuinely distinct assessable points in that
reasoning.
Do not choose a desired mark total first and then split, repeat or reword ideas simply to create
additional marks.
MARK SCHEME
After the 6 questions, provide a student-friendly mark scheme.
For each question:
• State the total number of marks available.
• Break the answer into the minimum distinct scientific reasoning points needed to answer the
question.
• Award individual marks to clear scientific points wherever appropriate.
• Show the minimum scientific reasoning chain required to reach the answer rather than only
giving the final answer.
• Keep each marking point concise and specific enough that a student can compare it directly
with their own answer.
• Only award separate marks for genuinely distinct pieces of scientific knowledge or reasoning.
• Do not create additional marking points by restating, rewording or artificially splitting the same
scientific idea.
• Each marking point should help a student identify a distinct part of their reasoning that was
present or missing.
• Do not unnecessarily combine several genuinely separate pieces of reasoning into one
marking point.
• Do not add scientific knowledge that was not required to answer the original question.
• Use scientifically precise wording that is consistent with the supplied revision content.
• Do not introduce stronger, different or less precise scientific claims than the supplied content
supports.
• Accept scientifically equivalent wording where appropriate.
Do NOT label marking points as "knowledge", "application", "recall" or similar. The mark scheme
should clearly expose the scientific reasoning required, but students should compare it with their
own answer and determine for themselves why any marks were lost.

A-level Biology AO2 application questions and a student-friendly mark scheme.
PURPOSE OF THE QUESTIONS
These questions will form the AO2 section of a mini-quiz completed after a student has revised
this page.
Their purpose is to give students controlled practice at USING and UNDERSTANDING the
biological knowledge they have just revised in different ways.
They should act as a stepping stone between recalling knowledge and later completing full
A-level exam questions.
They are NOT intended to reproduce the exact distribution or difficulty of AO2 questions found
in an exam. For example, even where a topic is commonly assessed through calculations or
complex data analysis in exams, these questions can still assess whether students understand
and can apply the underlying biology.
WHAT THE QUESTIONS SHOULD ASSESS
Focus primarily on students applying the biological ideas, processes and content they have just
revised.
Treat anything taught on the supplied page as part of the content. For example, if the page
teaches a practical method, experimental technique, procedure or working-scientifically idea,
students can be asked to apply it.
Do not require substantial biological knowledge from other topics that is not contained in the
supplied page.
Questions may place the revised knowledge into:
• theoretical contexts — applying biological knowledge to a scientific situation, problem or
mechanism;
• practical contexts — applying knowledge to an experimental method, technique or procedure.
Questions may also involve:
• qualitative information — observations, descriptions, results or other non-numerical
information;
• quantitative information — measurements, numerical information, simple calculations or
numerical relationships.
Use these where they genuinely suit the supplied content. Do not force an equal number of
theoretical/practical or qualitative/quantitative questions.
QUANTITATIVE INFORMATION AND MATHS
Maths, equations, numerical information, graphs or data may be used where they naturally help
students apply or understand the biology they have just revised.
Brief calculations are allowed.
However, do not create questions whose main challenge is carrying out a mathematical
procedure rather than applying the biological or practical understanding taught on the page.
For example, using numerical information to predict, compare, explain or make sense of a
biological situation is particularly appropriate.
APPLICATION TYPES
Create genuine AO2, not recall questions. A student should have to take something they have
learned from the page and USE it in a new situation, rather than simply repeat, state or describe
what the page says.
Use the following forms of application where they genuinely fit the content:
• Novel context — apply the knowledge to a new example or biological situation.
• Develop further — use a learned biological principle or mechanism to work out something that
was not explicitly taught.
• Make sense of information — use the learned biology to understand, interpret or explain
information supplied in the question.
• Make links — connect two or more biological ideas where the knowledge required for the link
is contained within the supplied revision content.
MECHANISMS AND PROCESSES
Where the page teaches a biological mechanism, pathway, sequence or process, consider
changing, inhibiting, removing or disrupting one component and asking the student to predict
and explain the downstream consequence.
For example, a question might change one stage of a pathway, prevent a molecule or structure
from functioning normally, or alter a condition affecting the process.
These questions should require the student to reason through the biological mechanism rather
than simply recall the sequence.
Do not overuse this structure. It is one form of application rather than the default form for every
question.
DIVERSITY OF APPLICATION
Select the application types and question structures that best suit the biological content rather
than forcing equal numbers of each.
Where the content allows, use at least 3 different forms of application across the 6 questions.
Do not force an application type where it does not fit naturally.
Vary both:
1. the biological knowledge being applied; and
2. what the student has to DO with that knowledge.
For example, the set might require students to predict a consequence, explain an unfamiliar
observation, work forwards through a mechanism, reason backwards from a result, compare
two situations, interpret supplied information, apply a practical principle or determine the effect
of changing a variable.
Avoid creating several questions that use essentially the same application structure, even if
different pieces of biological knowledge are being tested.
For example, do not repeatedly change or inhibit a different stage of the same pathway and ask
the student to explain the downstream effect.
Avoid testing the same biological relationship in essentially the same way more than once.
GUARDRAIL AGAINST NEAR-AO1 QUESTIONS
Some questions may involve relatively small amounts of transfer and therefore sit close to AO1.
This is acceptable provided the student still has to genuinely apply the knowledge.
However, across the set, avoid relying too heavily on questions where a short context simply
describes a structure, process or function and the student identifies or recalls the corresponding
biological term.
In particular, factual textbook pages should not result mainly in questions that simply ask
students to identify a structure from its function, identify a process from its description, or repeat
a memorised structure-function relationship.
Where possible, use factual knowledge as the starting point for a prediction, explanation,
comparison or consequence.
STEPPING-STONE DIFFICULTY
These should be stepping-stone A-level AO2 questions rather than full exam-level AO2
questions.
Each question should:
• require genuine application of the revised biological knowledge;
• use a concise and clearly presented context;
• normally require only one or two main application/reasoning steps;
• make the relevant biological idea reasonably identifiable;
• contain little or no irrelevant information;
• be answerable primarily using the content just revised;
• use appropriate A-level biological terminology;
• allow students to demonstrate understanding without the additional complexity of a full exam
question.
Some questions may be relatively close to AO1, provided the student still has to apply the
knowledge to a new situation. Others can require greater transfer.
There does not need to be a fixed progression in difficulty from Question 1 to Question 6.
Do not make questions more difficult simply by:
• making the context longer;
• adding irrelevant information;
• requiring substantial synoptic knowledge from other topics;
• requiring unnecessarily complex mathematical processing;
• or creating unnecessarily long reasoning chains.
The aim is to preserve genuine A-level AO2 application while reducing the contextual, synoptic,
mathematical and multi-step complexity students are likely to encounter in full exam questions.
MARK ALLOCATION
Use an appropriate mark allocation for each question based on the minimum biological
reasoning required.
Most questions should normally be worth 2–4 marks.
Do not increase the mark allocation simply to make a question appear more A-level.
STUDENT-FRIENDLY MARK SCHEME
After the 6 questions, provide a student-friendly mark scheme.
For each question:
• show the question number and total marks;
• break the answer into the minimum scientific reasoning chain required;
• present each distinct marking point separately;
• show [1] beside each marking point;
• keep the wording clear enough for a student to compare directly with their own answer;
• include the biological links needed to earn the marks rather than only giving the final answer.
Do not rigidly label marking points as “knowledge”, “application” or “answer”. Biological
reasoning may combine these.
The mark scheme should make it possible for a student to identify exactly where their reasoning
chain became incomplete or incorrect.
Do not award separate marks twice for essentially the same biological idea.
OUTPUT
Create exactly 6 questions followed by the student-friendly mark scheme.
Use a mixture of appropriate question styles such as predict, explain, suggest, compare,
interpret and determine where these suit the content.
Do not use multiple-choice questions.
Keep contexts and questions concise.
Do not identify the application type, context type or information type beside each question.
Do not explain why you selected each question.
AO3
GCSE
Using only the scientific knowledge contained in the supplied textbook screenshot, create 3
GCSE Science AO3 questions and a student-friendly mark scheme.
PURPOSE OF THE QUESTIONS
These questions will form the AO3 section of a mini-quiz completed after a student has revised
this page.
Their purpose is to give students controlled practice at ANALYSING, INTERPRETING and
EVALUATING scientific information and evidence.
They should act as a stepping stone between revising scientific content and later completing full
exam questions containing AO3.
The questions should therefore practise genuine AO3 thinking while reducing the additional
difficulty created by long, complex or information-heavy exam stimuli.
They are NOT intended to reproduce the exact distribution, length or difficulty of AO3 questions
found in an exam.
WHAT THE QUESTIONS SHOULD ASSESS
Use the supplied textbook page as the scientific knowledge base for the questions.
Students may be given new results, observations, data, experimental situations, claims or other
information related to this knowledge and asked to analyse or evaluate them.
Do not require substantial scientific knowledge from other topics that is not contained in the
supplied page.
Questions should primarily assess one or more of the following AO3 types:
1. INTERPRET EVIDENCE
Student analyses supplied information and identifies a meaningful pattern, relationship,
difference or implication.
Possible stimuli include graphs, tables, results, diagrams or observations.
2. EXPLAIN EVIDENCE
Student uses scientific understanding to explain a supplied result, pattern, difference or
observation.
Possible stimuli include results, observations, graphs, tables, diagrams or experimental
contexts.
3. PREDICT / INFER
Student uses supplied evidence to determine something that is not directly stated or shown.
Possible stimuli include graphs, tables, results, trends, observations, diagrams or scenarios.
4. EVALUATE AN INVESTIGATION
Student analyses how an investigation was carried out to identify limitations, evaluate the quality
of the evidence, or suggest justified improvements.
This may include controls and variables, errors and limitations, improvements, reliability,
uncertainty, validity or representativeness.
Possible stimuli include experimental methods, apparatus, results, graphs, tables or sampling
procedures.
5. JUDGE A CONCLUSION / CLAIM
Student analyses evidence to decide whether, or to what extent, it supports a stated conclusion
or claim.
Possible stimuli include a claim combined with results, graphs, tables, experimental evidence,
written scientific information or competing evidence.
QUESTION SELECTION
Create 3 questions that provide useful AO3 practice for the scientific content on the supplied
page.
Before selecting the questions, consider the opportunities provided by the content for ALL FIVE
AO3 types.
Select the question types that provide the most useful and natural AO3 practice for that
particular content.
Do NOT force coverage of particular AO3 types simply to create variety.
However, do NOT default to INTERPRET EVIDENCE or JUDGE A CONCLUSION / CLAIM
simply because these can easily be constructed using generated tables or claims.
Variation in the selected AO3 types across different scientific topics is expected.
Give particular consideration to EXPLAIN EVIDENCE and PREDICT / INFER when the supplied
content contains a mechanism, process or cause-and-effect relationship that could be used to
make sense of an unfamiliar result or observation.
Avoid producing multiple questions that assess essentially the same AO3 reasoning when other
useful AO3 opportunities are available.
The questions may use different AO3 types or may combine closely related AO3 reasoning
where this creates a useful question.
Questions should primarily assess AO3. A small amount of AO2 reasoning may be required
where students need to use their recently revised scientific knowledge to interpret, explain or
evaluate the supplied evidence. Do not allow the AO2 demand to become the main challenge of
the question.
STEPPING-STONE DIFFICULTY
Isolate the important AO3 thinking as much as possible.
Keep unfamiliar information concise and accessible.
Use the minimum amount of information needed for the student to perform the intended AO3
reasoning.
A question should not become difficult simply because the student has to process a large
amount of unfamiliar information.
Prefer one clear AO3 reasoning task over several different AO3 demands bundled into one
complex exam-style question.
The questions should still require genuine analysis, interpretation, evaluation, judgement or
inference. Do not make them easier by turning them into AO1 recall or straightforward AO2
application questions.
AO3 TYPE-SPECIFIC REQUIREMENTS
For INTERPRET EVIDENCE questions:
Ensure the task requires students to determine what the evidence MEANS rather than merely
copy values or state an immediately obvious trend.
Identifying a pattern may form part of the reasoning, but where the revised content allows,
require students to use that pattern to reach a scientific interpretation, comparison or
implication.
For EXPLAIN EVIDENCE questions:
Provide an unfamiliar result, observation, pattern or difference that students can explain using
the scientific understanding they have just revised.
The evidence should create something meaningful for the student to explain rather than simply
restating a fact from the textbook page.
For PREDICT / INFER questions:
The prediction or inference must genuinely arise from the supplied evidence.
Do not classify a question as Predict / Infer when the stimulus merely provides a context and the
answer is obtained mainly by applying recalled scientific knowledge.
The evidence should allow the student to determine something that is not directly stated or
shown.
For EVALUATE AN INVESTIGATION questions:
Ensure the supplied method, results or experimental information contains enough information
for the intended limitation, control, improvement or other evaluation to be identified.
Do not explicitly tell students the problem they are supposed to evaluate.
For JUDGE A CONCLUSION / CLAIM questions:
Avoid claims that are directly confirmed or contradicted by a single sentence in the stimulus.
Where appropriate, provide more than one relevant piece of evidence so the student must
weigh the evidence or determine whether the claim is fully, partly or not supported.
The supplied evidence must be sufficient for a reasoned judgement.
STIMULUS USE
Use a stimulus when it is needed to create genuine AO3 reasoning.
Do NOT add a graph, table, diagram, experimental method or other stimulus merely to make a
question look more like an exam question.
A stimulus may be as simple as:
- a short observation;
- a small set of results;
- a short scientific scenario;
- a student's or scientist's claim;
- a brief experimental method.
Where useful, you may also create:
- numerical data;
- a small results table;
- written experimental information.
Follow these four stimulus rules:
RULE 1 — USE THE SIMPLEST FORMAT THAT PRESERVES THE AO3 REASONING
Use the simplest stimulus format capable of presenting the evidence needed for the intended
AO3 reasoning.
Do not convert information into a table, graph or diagram when a short written observation or
description would allow the student to perform exactly the same reasoning.
For example, if the only relevant information is that a substance does not conduct electricity
when solid but does conduct when molten, this can be presented as a short observation rather
than an unnecessary table.
However, simplicity of presentation must NOT remove part of the intended AO3 thinking.
RULE 2 — INCLUDE ONLY INFORMATION THAT CONTRIBUTES TO THE REASONING
Every variable, value, category or piece of contextual information included in the stimulus
should have a purpose in the intended AO3 reasoning.
Do not add variables such as year, trial number, group number or other contextual information
unless students need them to analyse, interpret or evaluate the evidence.
Reduce unnecessary information rather than reducing the scientific reasoning required.
RULE 3 — DO NOT STATE THE FEATURE THE STUDENT IS SUPPOSED TO IDENTIFY
Do not simplify a stimulus by explicitly telling students the pattern, difference, flaw, relationship
or other feature that they are supposed to identify or interpret.
If extracting information from a graph, diagram, experimental setup, method or set of results is
itself part of the intended AO3 reasoning, preserve that task for the student.
For example, if students should compare the gradients of two lines on a graph, do not replace
the graph with a sentence telling them which line is steeper.
Similarly, if students should identify an uncontrolled variable from an experimental setup, do not
explicitly state which variable was different.
In these cases, specify the graph, diagram or other stimulus that is required, even if it cannot
currently be rendered.
RULE 4 — THE STIMULUS MUST CREATE THE AO3 DEMAND, NOT MERELY PROVIDE AN
AO2 CONTEXT
The student should genuinely need to analyse, interpret or evaluate the supplied evidence to
answer the question.
Do not create a stimulus that merely places recalled scientific knowledge into an unfamiliar
context while the answer is obtained mainly through straightforward application of that
knowledge.
This is particularly important for PREDICT / INFER questions.
Ask whether removing the evidence would substantially remove the reasoning required to reach
the answer. If the student could answer essentially the same question from recalled knowledge
alone, reconsider whether the question genuinely assesses AO3.
STIMULI THAT CANNOT CURRENTLY BE PRODUCED
You may specify a graph, diagram, image or other stimulus even if you cannot currently produce
it.
When this occurs, DO NOT avoid the question or replace it with a weaker question simply
because the stimulus cannot currently be rendered.
Instead, clearly describe exactly what stimulus would be required.
For a graph, provide:
- the graph type;
- x-axis variable and units;
- y-axis variable and units;
- all data required to construct it;
- any lines/groups/categories that should appear.
For a diagram or image, provide:
- what should be shown;
- the scientifically important features;
- any labels required;
- which features must be compared or interpreted by the student.
For an experimental setup, describe the apparatus/setup and any scientifically important
features that would need to be represented.
Do not include unnecessary visual detail.
IMPORTANT: The purpose of this beta is to identify the best AO3 questions AND the stimuli
required to support them. Do not restrict question selection according to current technical
stimulus-generation limitations.
QUESTION DESIGN
Questions should be concise and clear.
Keep reading demand lower than a typical full exam question wherever possible.
Use accessible unfamiliar contexts rather than complex exam-style scenarios.
Do not give away the scientific reasoning required to answer the question.
Do not simply ask students to recall something stated on the textbook page.
Do not create questions where the answer depends mainly on guessing.
Where students evaluate evidence or a conclusion, ensure the supplied information contains
enough evidence for them to make a reasoned judgement.
Where students evaluate an investigation, ensure the method contains enough information for
the intended limitation, control, improvement or other evaluation to be identified.
Where students interpret data, ensure the data contain a meaningful feature that actually
requires interpretation rather than simple extraction of a number.
Where students predict or infer, ensure the answer follows reasonably from the supplied
evidence rather than requiring unsupported speculation.
STIMULUS ACCURACY
Any generated results, data, methods, claims or other stimuli must be scientifically plausible and
consistent with the scientific knowledge on the supplied page.
Do not introduce additional scientific knowledge that students would need to have revised
elsewhere.
Generated data should contain only the complexity needed for the intended question.
Generated data should make the intended scientific relationship accessible, but should not
always be perfectly proportional or artificially obvious.
Include only as much variation or complexity as is useful for the intended AO3 reasoning.
Do not create artificial anomalies, differences or relationships unless they have a clear purpose
in the AO3 reasoning being assessed.
OUTPUT FORMAT
Produce TWO clearly separated sections.
SECTION 1 — STUDENT OUTPUT
This section should contain ONLY what a student needs to complete the questions.
Do NOT display:
- the AO3 taxonomy type;
- the words "AO3 type";
- beta-testing notes;
- stimulus-generation notes;
- explanations of why a stimulus was selected.
For each question, provide:

Keep the mark scheme student-friendly and directly matched to each question.

A-LEVEL BIOLOGY AO3 MINI-QUIZ GENERATION PROMPT
Using only the biological knowledge contained in the supplied textbook screenshot/page(s),
create 6 A-level Biology AO3 questions and a student-friendly mark scheme.
PURPOSE OF THE QUESTIONS
These questions will form the AO3 section of a mini-quiz completed after a student has revised
the supplied textbook content.
Their purpose is to give students controlled practice at ANALYSING, INTERPRETING and
EVALUATING scientific information and evidence.
They should act as a stepping stone between revising biological content and later completing
full A-level Biology exam questions containing AO3.
The questions should therefore practise genuine AO3 thinking while reducing the additional
difficulty created by long, complex or information-heavy exam stimuli.
They are NOT intended to reproduce the exact distribution, length or difficulty of AO3 questions
found in an exam.
A-level Biology contains substantial AO3 demand, so the quiz should provide sufficient breadth
and quantity of AO3 practice rather than reducing the section to only a small number of
questions.
SOURCE-KNOWLEDGE RULE
Use the supplied textbook page(s) as the biological knowledge base for the questions.
Do not require substantial biological knowledge from other topics that is not contained in the
supplied material.
Students may be given new results, observations, data, experimental situations, scientific claims
or other information related to the supplied biological knowledge and asked to analyse or
evaluate them.
The STIMULUS may therefore contain new evidence or an unfamiliar situation, but the
BIOLOGICAL KNOWLEDGE needed to reason about it must come from the supplied textbook
content.
NUMBER AND STRUCTURE
Create exactly 6 questions.
Use approximately 2–3 stimuli across the six questions.
DEFAULT STRUCTURE:
- 3 stimuli
- 2 questions based on each stimulus
This is preferred because it gives students sufficient AO3 practice while allowing them to reason
about a piece of evidence in more than one way.
However, do not force exactly two questions onto every stimulus if a different 2–3 stimulus
arrangement produces substantially better AO3 questions.
Questions sharing a stimulus should normally assess DIFFERENT reasoning demands rather
than asking essentially the same thing twice.
For example, one question might ask the student to interpret the evidence and a second might
ask them to explain the pattern, evaluate a conclusion or assess the method.
WHAT THE QUESTIONS SHOULD ASSESS
Questions should primarily assess one or more of the following AO3 types:
1. INTERPRET EVIDENCE
Identify patterns, trends, differences, relationships or other meaningful features in results or
observations.
2. EXPLAIN EVIDENCE
Use relevant biological knowledge from the supplied textbook content to explain a pattern,
difference or result shown in the stimulus.
3. DRAW A CONCLUSION
Use the evidence provided to reach an appropriate scientific conclusion.
4. EVALUATE EVIDENCE OR A CLAIM
Judge how well results support a conclusion, hypothesis, explanation or scientific claim.
5. EVALUATE OR IMPROVE A METHOD
Identify limitations in how evidence was obtained and explain how an investigation could be
improved or how stronger evidence could be obtained.
6. CONSIDER ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS
Recognise when evidence could have more than one explanation, when another variable may
account for a result, or when the evidence is insufficient to establish causation.
Not every quiz needs to contain every AO3 type.
Choose the AO3 demands that are most appropriate to the biological content and available
evidence.
Across the six questions, aim for a MIX of AO3 reasoning rather than repeatedly assessing the
same skill.
THE AO3 GATE
Every question must require meaningful use of information contained in its stimulus.
Apply this test to EVERY proposed question:
"If the stimulus were removed, could a well-prepared student answer essentially the same
question from biological recall alone?"
If YES, redesign the question.
A question is not genuinely AO3 simply because a context, table, graph or experiment has been
placed before it.
The information in the stimulus must actually be necessary to answer the question fully.
For example:
WEAK:
"Explain how competitive inhibition affects enzyme activity."
This is primarily recall/application of biological knowledge.
STRONGER:
"The results show that the difference between the inhibited and uninhibited reaction becomes
smaller as substrate concentration increases. Use the results and your knowledge of
competitive inhibition to explain this pattern."
Here the student must first engage with the evidence and then use biological knowledge to
explain it.
Similarly, questions that ask students to evaluate a claim must provide evidence against which
that claim can genuinely be assessed.
Questions about experimental methods must contain enough methodological information for the
student to identify a real limitation or improvement rather than simply recalling generic
experimental precautions.
QUESTION DEMAND
Keep the AO3 reasoning genuine, but keep the stimulus and wording controlled and accessible.
The challenge should come primarily from WHAT THE STUDENT HAS TO DO WITH THE
EVIDENCE, not from decoding an unnecessarily complicated stimulus.
Questions should generally be short and focused.
Most questions should be worth approximately 2–4 marks.
Avoid turning the mini-quiz into a set of large exam-style extended-response questions.
Simple description of a pattern or trend may be used as an introductory AO3 question, but
should not dominate the quiz.
Across the six questions, MOST questions should require students to go beyond describing
what the evidence shows by doing something further with it, such as:
- explaining the evidence;
- evaluating it;
- drawing and justifying a conclusion;
- assessing a scientific claim;
- considering the strength or limitations of the evidence;
- identifying an alternative explanation;
- or evaluating/improving how the evidence was obtained.
Where an "explain" question is used, it should normally require the student to:
1. identify or use a relevant feature of the stimulus; and
2. use biological knowledge to account for that feature.
Do not allow an explain question to become a standard AO2 question that merely happens to sit
underneath a stimulus.
STIMULUS DESIGN
Use only stimuli that are necessary for the AO3 reasoning being assessed.
Suitable stimulus formats include:
- numerical results tables;
- graphs;
- short experimental descriptions;
- observations;
- scientific claims or conclusions;
- hypotheses;
- comparisons between treatments or conditions;
- short sets of results;
- combinations of the above.
Keep stimuli:
- minimal;
- purposeful;
- scientifically clear;
- easy to read;
- and no more complex than necessary for the AO3 skill being assessed.
Do not add irrelevant contextual information simply to make a question appear more exam-like.
Do not make all stimuli the same format where another format would assess the intended
reasoning more effectively.
Choose the format because it is the clearest way of presenting the evidence required by the
question.
DIAGRAM / IMAGE RULE
DO NOT use biological diagrams, biological illustrations, generated biological images,
apparatus diagrams or other image-based biological stimuli.
Do not create questions that depend on the AI accurately generating or reproducing a biological
diagram.
If the biological idea could be tested using a diagram, redesign it using another stimulus format
such as:
- a graph;
- a numerical table;
- observations;
- a short experimental description;
- or a scientific claim.
Graphs are permitted because they represent quantitative data rather than requiring generation
of a biological illustration.
GRAPH RULE
Use a graph only when the graphical representation itself contributes meaningfully to the AO3
reasoning.
For example, a graph may be appropriate when students need to interpret:
- a trend;
- a plateau;
- an optimum;
- a change in gradient;
- differences between curves;
- or relationships between continuous variables.
If students only need to compare a few exact numerical values, a table may be clearer and
more efficient.
Do not use a graph simply for visual variety.
DATA AND NUMERACY
Numerical data may be created where appropriate.
Keep numbers simple enough that the task remains focused on biological AO3 reasoning rather
than substantial mathematical processing.
Students may:
- compare values;
- identify increases/decreases;
- recognise patterns;
- calculate simple differences or proportions where useful;
- or use quantitative evidence to support a conclusion.
Do not introduce substantial calculations unless the supplied textbook content specifically
makes that mathematical skill relevant.
SCIENTIFIC CLAIMS AND CONCLUSIONS
Where useful, provide a short scientific claim, hypothesis or conclusion for the student to
assess.
Claims should be designed so that students must compare them with the supplied evidence.
Where appropriate, evidence may:
- support the claim;
- partly support it;
- contradict part of it;
- or be insufficient to establish it fully.
Avoid making every claim obviously completely correct or completely incorrect.
Encourage scientifically cautious reasoning.
Students should distinguish between:
- evidence supporting a conclusion;
- evidence being consistent with a conclusion;
- and evidence proving that conclusion.
Do not imply causation where the evidence only demonstrates an association.
METHOD EVALUATION
Method-evaluation questions should be specific to the investigation described.
Avoid generic questions that can be answered with stock phrases such as:
"repeat the experiment",
"calculate a mean",
or
"control variables"
without engaging with the actual evidence.
If repeats are relevant, students should ideally explain WHY repeats would strengthen the
evidence.
If a confounding variable is relevant, students should identify the biologically meaningful variable
and explain HOW it affects interpretation of the results.
Method improvements should connect directly to a limitation in the evidence.
QUESTION VARIETY
Avoid producing six questions with essentially the same structure.
A strong set might, for example, include:
- interpretation of a pattern;
- biological explanation of that pattern;
- evaluation of a claim using numerical evidence;
- explanation of a difference between treatments;
- drawing a justified conclusion;
- evaluation or improvement of an investigation.
This is an example, not a compulsory template.
Choose the combination that best fits the supplied biological content.
STUDENT-FRIENDLY MARK SCHEME
After the six questions, provide a mark scheme.
For each question:
- state the number of marks;
- give clear bullet-point marking points;
- make each marking point specific enough for a student to understand what earns the mark;
- use biological terminology appropriate for A-level;
- explicitly include use of stimulus evidence where the question requires it;
- accept scientifically equivalent wording where appropriate.
Where useful, include a brief "Reasoning to learn" statement explaining the AO3 thinking the
student should take from the question.
For example:
Reasoning to learn: State the conclusion, then support it with specific evidence.
Reasoning to learn: Start with what the results show, then use biological knowledge to explain
the pattern.
Reasoning to learn: Link the limitation in the method to how it affects the strength of the
evidence.
Do not make these statements repetitive or include them where they add little value.
FINAL AO3 QUALITY CHECK
Before producing the final output, check all six questions individually.
For each question confirm internally that:
1. The stimulus is NECESSARY to answer the question fully.
2. The question is primarily AO3 rather than AO1 or AO2.
3. The required biological knowledge is supported by the supplied textbook page(s).
4. The stimulus is minimal and purposeful.
5. The question does not depend on a biological diagram or generated biological image.
6. The mark scheme requires engagement with the evidence where appropriate.
7. The question is not simply duplicating the reasoning assessed by another question.
8. The wording is accessible enough that stimulus complexity does not obscure the AO3 skill.
Then check the quiz as a whole:
- exactly 6 questions;
- approximately 2–3 stimuli;
- normally 3 stimuli with 2 questions per stimulus;
- sufficient variety of AO3 reasoning;
- most questions go beyond simple description of evidence;
- no question is primarily AO2;
- no biological diagrams or generated biological images are required.
BETA / DEVELOPER SPECIFICATION
For the current version, the AI does NOT need to create or render the final stimuli itself.
Instead, after the student questions and mark scheme, provide a separate section titled:
BETA / DEVELOPER SPECIFICATION
For each stimulus, state:
1. STIMULUS NUMBER
2. QUESTIONS USING IT
For example: Q1 and Q2.
3. STIMULUS FORMAT
For example:
- graph;
- numerical results table;
- experimental method + results;
- scientific claim + data.
4. EXACT STIMULUS CONTENT
Provide all information required for the stimulus to be generated later.
For a table, provide the complete data.
For a written stimulus, provide the exact wording.
For a graph, provide a COMPLETE GRAPH GENERATION SPECIFICATION including:
- graph type;
- x-axis variable and units;
- y-axis variable and units;
- axis ranges where appropriate;
- all numerical data points;
- number and names of data series;
- whether a legend is required;
- and any other information required to reproduce the graph accurately.
Do not include annotations that reveal the answer to the questions.
5. WHY THE STIMULUS IS NECESSARY
Briefly explain what information students must extract from the stimulus in order to answer the
associated question(s).
6. WHY THIS FORMAT WAS CHOSEN
Briefly explain why a graph, table, written investigation or other format is the clearest and most
efficient representation for the intended AO3 reasoning.
Finally include an:
AO3 TYPE CHECK
List Q1-Q6 and identify the primary AO3 demand of each question.
Also state:
- total number of stimuli;
- which questions share each stimulus;
- stimulus formats used;
- confirmation that every stimulus is necessary, minimal, purposeful and clear;
- confirmation that every question requires information from its stimulus;
- confirmation that no question is primarily AO2;
- confirmation that no biological diagram, illustration or generated biological image is required.
IMPORTANT DEVELOPMENT NOTE
The current prompt is being tested in a workflow where the AI specifies the stimuli but does not
need to produce/render all of them itself.
In the final developer implementation, stimulus generation/rendering will be introduced
separately.
The developer should therefore use the BETA / DEVELOPER SPECIFICATION as the
structured source from which graphs, tables and written stimuli can be generated accurately.
The question-generation system should determine WHAT evidence is needed and WHY before
the rendering system determines HOW that stimulus is presented.
Do not weaken or simplify the AO3 reasoning merely because a stimulus will later need to be
generated programmatically.
OUTPUT ORDER
Produce the output in this order:
1. Title/topic
2. Student instructions
3. Stimulus 1 + associated question(s)
4. Stimulus 2 + associated question(s)
5. Stimulus 3 if required + associated question(s)
6. Student-Friendly Mark Scheme
7. BETA / DEVELOPER SPECIFICATION
8. AO3 TYPE CHECK
